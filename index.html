Getting started with FCH₄ and FN₂O

A short guide for adding methane and nitrous oxide to eddy-covariance workflows

This site is a public, getting-started package for researchers who want to add eddy-covariance methane (FCH₄) and nitrous oxide (FN₂O) fluxes to their work. It is written mainly for prospective and early-career flux scientists: graduate students, postdocs, and new principal investigators planning their first non-CO₂ flux system.

It draws on materials assembled by the FLUXNET-Coop CH₄/N₂O Data Processing Committee. Jump to the guide →

Start with ICOS for the full protocol. This site is a short complement to the official ICOS guidance, not a replacement for it. The ICOS Ecosystem Instructions for turbulent flux measurements of CH₄ and N₂O remain the detailed reference for instrument setup, maintenance, calibration, and ICOS data submission.

  • ICOS instructions for CH₄ and N₂O eddy-covariance fluxes
  • Nicolini, G., Sabbatini, S., Mammarella, I., Nemitz, E. & Papale, D. (2024). ICOS Ecosystem Instructions for turbulent flux measurements of CH₄ and N₂O. doi:10.18160/61J9-4MMK

What you will find here is shorter and more informal: why these gases behave differently from CO₂, what to think about first, which processing steps tend to matter most, which open tools people already use, and where the science is still being worked out.

Why a separate getting-started guide?

Eddy-covariance methods for carbon dioxide and energy are comparatively mature. CH₄ and N₂O fluxes are different in ways that matter for processing. They are often smaller and noisier, and they can be episodic: N₂O can pulse after fertilizer and rain, and CH₄ can be released in short bursts. Those properties change how you:

  • detect the time lag between the gas analyzer and the sonic anemometer,
  • filter spikes and outliers without deleting real emission events,
  • decide whether a friction-velocity (u*) threshold developed for CO₂ should be applied,
  • fill gaps in the half-hourly record, and
  • report uncertainty.

New groups should not have to reconstruct that advice from scattered papers and meeting notes. This site collects the committee’s public-facing guidance in one place and points you to ICOS and the literature whenever you need the full specification.

Mission

The committee’s mission is to provide accessible guidelines for new investigators, support the inclusive growth of the FLUXNET CH₄ and N₂O community, improve the standardization of data processing, and, over the longer term, contribute reference practices that can inform measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) uses of these fluxes.

Four objectives

  1. Better quality control. Improve quality control and quality assurance for CH₄ and N₂O fluxes, both before half-hourly fluxes are computed (high-frequency, raw-data processing) and afterward (half-hourly QC/QA).
  2. Open resources. Share open resources for instrument setup and for reproducible processing.
  3. Useful ancillary data. Identify the ancillary measurements that most help people interpret, gap-fill, and compare CH₄ and N₂O fluxes.
  4. Practical guidance for new PIs. Offer best-practice guidance on site selection, systems and maintenance, ancillary measurements, high-frequency preprocessing, half-hourly post-processing, and how this work connects to global FLUXNET science.

How the guide is organized

High-frequency data

From the analyzer and sonic anemometer to 30-minute fluxes: time lag, despiking, spectral corrections, and quality flags.

Half-hourly processing

QC/QA of 30-minute output, the open question of u* filtering, gap-filling, and uncertainty.

Ancillary variables

The extra measurements that make CH₄ and N₂O time series interpretable: water, redox, soils, vegetation, and management.

How this relates to ICOS

If you run or plan an ICOS station, start with the ICOS instruction and discuss site-specific choices with the ICOS Ecosystem Thematic Centre. The ICOS instruction itself builds on:

  • Nemitz, E., Mammarella, I., Ibrom, A., et al. (2018). Standardisation of eddy-covariance flux measurements of methane and nitrous oxide. International Agrophysics 32, 517–549. doi:10.1515/intag-2017-0042
  • Mammarella, I., et al. (2020). Protocol for non-CO₂ eddy covariance measurements, QA/QC, data processing and gap-filling. RINGO H2020 Project Deliverable D3.5. Available from the ICOS RINGO deliverables page.

This site deliberately does not repeat ICOS rules about when CH₄ or N₂O measurements are mandatory at Class 1 stations, approved sensors, inlet geometry, calibration schedules, or file submission. It is meant to sit beside that guidance and help people getting started anywhere in the FLUXNET community.

A note on open questions

Not everything is settled. Several important choices are not yet standardized for non-CO₂ fluxes. Two big ones are whether a CO₂-derived u* threshold should be applied to FCH₄ and FN₂O, and how random, u*, and gap-filling uncertainties should be combined when fluxes are aggregated to daily, seasonal, or annual totals. The guide says so plainly and points to current options rather than inventing a single “correct” recipe.
