High-frequency data collection and processing

From the analyzer and sonic anemometer to 30-minute fluxes

Getting started

High-frequency processing is everything that turns raw sonic-anemometer and gas-analyzer records into a 30-minute flux. The building blocks you may know from CO₂ still apply: coordinate rotation, time-lag correction, detrending or high-pass filtering, air-density (WPL) terms where needed, spectral corrections, and quality flags. CH₄ and N₂O change the emphasis because the signal is often weaker and more intermittent.

For the full system-setup specification (sensors, inlet geometry, calibration, maintenance), see the ICOS CH₄/N₂O instruction and Nemitz et al. (2018). Raw-data processing for CO₂ and energy fluxes is covered in Sabbatini et al. (2018), doi:10.1515/intag-2017-0043. This page covers the issues that most often surprise people new to non-CO₂ fluxes.

Instruments and setup

These are complementary notes, not a sensor list.

  • Closed-path laser spectrometers are widely used for both CH₄ and N₂O. Pay attention to tube length, flow rate, and how you will find the time lag relative to the sonic anemometer.
  • Open-path CH₄ analyzers (including the LI-COR LI-7700) involve a well-known trade-off: open-path systems need larger air-density corrections, while closed-path systems can lose high-frequency signal in the tube under some conditions. See Detto et al. (2011), Agricultural and Forest Meteorology, doi:10.1016/j.agrformet.2011.05.014.
  • Network rules come first. As a matter of ICOS network policy, ICOS no longer accepts the LI-7700 at ICOS Class 1 ecosystem stations. If you are part of ICOS, follow ICOS. If you are not, treat instrument choice as a scientific and practical decision, follow your own network’s rules, and document your reasoning. This site does not ban any commercial sensor.
  • Record what you will need later: gas mole fractions, the three wind components, sonic temperature, and the diagnostics your analyzer provides (signal strength, flow, cell or mirror status, and so on).

Time lag

For closed-path systems, detecting the time delay between the sonic anemometer and the analyzer is one of the most important high-frequency steps, especially when fluxes are small. When the signal-to-noise ratio is low, a lag search over a wide window tends to lock onto noise and can bias fluxes. Langford et al. (2015), Atmospheric Measurement Techniques, doi:10.5194/amt-8-4197-2015, is a good introduction to time-lag determination and detection limits for low signal-to-noise eddy-covariance data.

Points emphasized in committee discussions:

  • Detect time lags carefully for closed-path laser spectrometers.
  • Use a predetermined, relatively narrow lag-search window together with a default (nominal) lag, rather than letting the search wander over a wide window at every interval.
  • High-pass filtered lag correction, as implemented in eddy4R, is one documented approach.

Wavelets and data retention

Conventional stationarity and turbulence flags can remove a large fraction of CH₄ or N₂O records at sites where fluxes arrive in short events. Wavelet-based methods are being used in this community to retain more high-quality data and to identify disruptive events, without treating every non-stationary period as unusable. Think of wavelets as a promising and actively used approach, not a required standard.

Despiking, spectral corrections, and quality flags

  • Despiking. Remove physically impossible spikes in the raw data before averaging, but be careful not to treat real emission pulses (ebullition, fertilizer bursts) as noise.
  • Spectral corrections. These can become large when tubes, filters, or analyzers attenuate high frequencies. Intervals with extreme correction factors are often filtered later, at the half-hourly stage.
  • Quality flags. Standard flux quality flags, such as the Mauder and Foken (2004) scheme based on steady-state and integral-turbulence-characteristics (SSITC) tests, are still commonly applied. Many groups keep quality classes 0 and 1 for quantitative work.

Software people use at this stage

EddyPro (LI-COR; the processing engine is open source) is the processing software most widely used in this community to compute 30-minute fluxes from raw files. Other groups use in-house MATLAB, R, or Python code, or research pipelines such as eddy4R.

Whatever you choose, write down your settings: the lag window and nominal lag, the despiking method, the spectral corrections, and whether a default CO₂-style outlier filter was left on.
