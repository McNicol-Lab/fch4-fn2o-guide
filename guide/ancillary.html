Ancillary variables

What to measure alongside CH₄ and N₂O fluxes

Getting started

Fluxes rarely explain themselves. CH₄ production, oxidation, and transport, and N₂O production and consumption, depend on water, oxygen (or redox state), temperature, substrate, vegetation, and, very often, human management. Ancillary measurements are also the predictors you will need for gap-filling and for comparing sites.

This page is a getting-started priority list distilled from committee discussions, including a working inventory of variables. It is not a mandatory protocol and not a substitute for ICOS or FLUXNET BADM metadata requirements. If you submit to a network, follow that network’s variable list (for ICOS, see the CH₄/N₂O instruction and the ICOS instructions for meteorological measurements, doi:10.18160/NHEG-4KWW). If you are just starting, measure the high-priority, feasible items for your ecosystem first.

The working inventory uses four priority levels:

  1. Important and usually feasible
  2. Important, but sometimes difficult
  3. Useful wishlist, usually feasible
  4. Useful wishlist, difficult

Items below are priority 1 unless marked otherwise.

CH₄: a strong starting set

Meteorology and footprint

  • Wind speed and wind direction (needed for the flux itself and to interpret the footprint)
  • Air pressure (influences gas release from soils)
  • Precipitation (related to wetness and water-table dynamics)

Soil and water

  • Soil temperature at several depths, and in the main landscape types inside the footprint
  • Soil moisture, ideally as a profile and in more than one patch type (TDR or similar)
  • Redox state, or another redox index (priority 2: important, often harder)
  • Water-table elevation above or below the ground surface (priority 2; manual wells or a calibrated pressure transducer)
  • Water or profile temperature
  • Dissolved oxygen in soil or porewater, where it can be measured

Vegetation and carbon inputs

  • Ecosystem type
  • Vegetation density, especially plants with aerenchyma (priority 2). Aerenchymatous plants can vent CH₄ past oxidizing layers; some mosses and floating plants can instead favor oxidation.
  • Plant growth forms (rooting depth, height, LAI)
  • Cover of plants, open soil, and open water, including patchiness and the length of plant–water edges (aerial imagery or classification helps)
  • Gross primary production and the CO₂ flux, which help describe carbon supply to methanogens

More static site descriptors

Often priority 3, but still worth collecting:

  • Soil texture
  • Soil carbon and nitrogen content

CH₄: extras that depend on the ecosystem

Marshes and tidal systems

  • Salinity (often essential where sulfate reduction competes with methanogenesis)
  • Tides and water-height variation; lateral carbon transport can be a large term

Peatlands

  • Drainage and rewetting
  • How much of the surface is ponded or affected by drainage (moisture topography)

Agricultural footprints

  • Manure type and amount, and how it is stored
  • Animal density and location in the footprint (enteric CH₄ as well as manure)
  • Animal diet, where it is known

N₂O: a strong starting set

N₂O work is especially common in agriculture, but low-nutrient and peatland sites matter too.

Environment

  • Air temperature, soil temperature, and soil moisture
  • Precipitation (often the trigger for emission pulses)
  • Albedo and snow cover or snow depth, when freeze–thaw emissions are possible

Soil nitrogen and physical properties

  • Soil nitrate (for example, KCl extracts) and ammonium
  • Soil type and organic-matter content, C:N ratio, bulk density, and texture

Management

Write these down even if you cannot measure them with sensors at high frequency.

  • Fertilizer type and formulation, including inhibitors (product names can hide formula changes)
  • Total nitrogen applied, and total ammoniacal nitrogen if the source is manure
  • Fertilization date, and the gap between fertilization and the next wetting event
  • Planting date, crop or plant type (including legumes), tillage, and application method
  • Irrigation timing
  • Harvest yields and plant nitrogen content, which help close an agronomic nitrogen budget

Other settings

  • In low-nutrient (oligotrophic) systems, regional ammonia concentrations or nitrogen deposition can matter even without fertilizer
  • Peatland drainage and land-use history
  • Dissolved oxygen in soil

How to use this list

What comes next

The ancillary-variables group has discussed a future white paper aimed at people entering CH₄ and N₂O eddy covariance. It would focus on terrestrial systems (agriculture, forests, tropics, peatlands, and other wet soils) and on the possible use of satellite products alongside tower data. Accompanying CO₂ fluxes are also useful for modeling. Until that paper exists, treat this page as a concise starting list rather than a final standard.
