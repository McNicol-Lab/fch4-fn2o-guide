Half-hourly processing

Quality control, u*, gap-filling, and uncertainty for 30-minute fluxes

Getting started

Once you have 30-minute FCH₄ or FN₂O, the post-processing chain is similar in outline to CO₂ work and different in the details. Committee discussions organized the chain this way:

  1. Quality-control the 30-minute output.
  2. Optionally, build the best available ancillary time series (see Ancillary variables).
  3. Address u*, gap-filling, and uncertainty.

The output of EddyPro (or an equivalent raw-data processor) is typically the starting file. Many groups then finish the workflow in R, Python, or MATLAB.

ICOS and the RINGO D3.5 protocol (Mammarella et al., 2020; ICOS RINGO deliverables) describe QA/QC, u* filtering, and gap-filling for CH₄ and N₂O in more depth. If you contribute to ICOS, follow the ICOS instruction. This page is a getting-started summary.

A practical QC/QA sequence

This sequence reflects common practice discussed by the committee. Thresholds are often site-specific. The goal is a transparent, stepwise procedure, not a single universal set of cut-offs.

  1. Start from well-computed fluxes. Use a predetermined, narrow lag-search window and a default (nominal) lag (see the high-frequency page).
  2. Remove only the physically impossible. Apply a coarse minimum/maximum filter only for physically impossible outliers. There is no universal “typical range” for CH₄ or N₂O; fluxes vary from near-zero uptake to large pulses, so set any bounds for your own site.
  3. Apply standard quality flags. Use the Mauder and Foken (2004) / SSITC flags. Many groups retain quality classes 0 and 1.
  4. Filter on diagnostics. Screen for incomplete records, low signal strength, implausible flux variances, closed-path flow rate, sonic pitch, implausible time lags, and unreasonably large spectral correction factors.
  5. Filter on wind direction where masts, buildings, or other obstacles contaminate part of the fetch.
  6. Propagate shared filters. Some filters apply across traces: if a period is rejected for one reason (for example a bad flow rate), remove every flux that depends on it.

Outliers and pulses

Because CH₄ and N₂O can be both very small and highly variable, it is easy to delete real events or to keep impossible spikes. Getting-started guidance from the committee:

  • Iterate, semi-automatically. Base the screening on the range you reasonably expect at your site and on the temporal character of the series: relatively steady, strongly seasonal, or pulsed.
  • Watch the defaults. At sites with low background fluxes plus short bursts (N₂O after fertilizer, or CH₄ ebullition), disable EddyPro’s default CO₂-oriented outlier filter. That default can remove exactly the events you care about.
  • Look at the data. After automatic screening, inspect the time series. A stepwise procedure plus quality flags is more defensible than a single hidden threshold.

Open tools people use for visualization and cleaning:

  • diive (Python)
  • Biomet.net and Calculation_Procedures (UBC Micromet)
  • A public wet-prairie processing workflow: atmos-flux-data

Useful papers: Vitale et al. (2020), Biogeosciences, on a robust data-cleaning procedure for eddy-covariance fluxes (doi:10.5194/bg-17-1367-2020); Langford et al. (2015), AMT (doi:10.5194/amt-8-4197-2015).

Friction velocity (u*)

Open question. Whether a u* threshold developed from CO₂ (NEE) should be applied to FCH₄ and FN₂O is not settled. Applying the CO₂-derived threshold is one option used in the community, but it is not established as correct for these gases.

A useful analogy comes from FLUXNET carbon and energy processing, where sensible and latent heat fluxes are not u*-filtered in the same way as NEE (Pastorello et al., 2020, Scientific Data, doi:10.1038/s41597-020-0534-3). CH₄ and N₂O may behave more like those energy fluxes at some sites and more like CO₂ at others.

What to do for now:

  • If you apply a u* filter, say so, show how the threshold was chosen, and consider reporting a sensitivity test.
  • If you do not apply one, say that too.

Gap-filling

Gap-filling here means imputing missing values in a single site-level time series. It is not interpolation in the casual sense, not forecasting, and not spatial upscaling.

Getting-started points from committee discussions:

  • Machine-learning ensembles are the current direction for community CH₄ products. Random forests and XGBoost are often sufficient; more elaborate architectures are not automatically better.
  • Community direction. The committee has committed to machine-learning gap-filling for FLUXNET-CH₄ version 2.0, with longer-term interest in aligning with ONEFlux.
  • Realistic validation. Use cross-validation that inserts artificial gaps similar in length and timing to the gaps you actually have, not only random point-wise holdouts.
  • Time predictors matter. At most sites, generic temporal predictors help: day of year or season encoded as sine and cosine terms, and other smooth temporal markers.
  • Pick metrics carefully. Choose error metrics that respect both short-term pulses and longer-term totals. A method that matches the mean but smears out events may look good on one score and poor on another.
  • Calibrated uncertainty. The uncertainty of filled values can be calibrated (for example with Platt scaling). Whether that calibrated error should replace a separately estimated random uncertainty is still being discussed.

About MDS. Marginal distribution sampling (MDS), as widely used for CO₂, is not automatically the best default for CH₄ or N₂O, especially when fluxes are pulsed or gaps are long. If you use MDS, document why.

Public tools and papers:

  • fluxgapfill — Irvin et al. (2021), Agricultural and Forest Meteorology 308–309, 108528, doi:10.1016/j.agrformet.2021.108528
  • methane-gapfill-ml
  • Kim et al. (2020), Global Change Biology, on gap-filling approaches for eddy-covariance CH₄ fluxes, doi:10.1111/gcb.14845
  • Moffat et al. (2007), Agricultural and Forest Meteorology, a classic comparison of CO₂ gap-filling methods and useful background, doi:10.1016/j.agrformet.2007.08.011

Uncertainty

For CO₂, community tools such as REddyProc already separate several uncertainty terms and show how to aggregate them (see the REddyProc aggregated-uncertainty vignette and the MPI-BGC FAQ).

For CH₄ and N₂O, the same ingredients are discussed, but they are not yet standardized:

  • Random measurement uncertainty, including serial correlation (Hollinger and Richardson, 2005, Tree Physiology, doi:10.1093/treephys/25.7.873).
  • Uncertainty from the choice of u* threshold, if a threshold is applied.
  • Uncertainty from gap-filling, for example the spread across models or across gap-filling runs.
  • How those terms combine when you report a daily, seasonal, or annual total.
  • How to combine CO₂, CH₄, and N₂O when someone wants a net greenhouse-gas budget. Adding variances is only valid if the errors are independent, so do not treat that as automatic.

A transparent first step is to report each term you can estimate and to say clearly which terms you could not yet quantify. There is no single official non-CO₂ uncertainty recipe yet.

Remaining questions

Still being worked out:

  • Are CO₂-style random-uncertainty estimators the best available for CH₄ and N₂O?
  • Is gap-filling uncertainty partly already contained in the spread across u* scenarios, or should it be estimated separately?
  • How should very long gaps (for example, longer than about three months, which MDS often will not fill) be handled?
  • How should uncertainties be communicated when people later compute net greenhouse-gas budgets?
